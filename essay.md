# Section 1 \- What are ideas

## Introduction

The modern understanding of ideas underwent a profound shift in 1976 with the publication of *The Selfish Gene*. In this work, Richard Dawkins reframed evolutionary theory by arguing that natural selection does not primarily act on species or organisms, but on genes themselves. Genes, he proposed, are units of inheritance stable enough to persist across generations, while organisms function as “survival machines” constructed by genes to ensure their continued replication. This gene‑centred view introduced the broader concept of the *replicator*: an entity that uses available resources to make copies of itself and can, in principle, endure across long stretches of time.

The most influential part of the book appears in chapter 11, where Dawkins extends the replicator concept beyond biology. He introduces the term *meme* to describe a unit of cultural transmission—an idea, symbol, or behaviour that spreads from mind to mind through imitation. Ideas, on this view, do not pass between individuals as fixed, immutable objects. Each person interprets them differently, reshaping them as they are transmitted. What persists is not the exact wording or form of an idea, but an underlying conceptual pattern that survives through repeated reinterpretation. If ideas behave like replicators—entities capable of being transmitted, transformed, and preserved across minds—then they can be studied not merely as abstractions, but as dynamic participants in cultural evolution, competing for scarce cognitive and social resources.

## 1.1 Interpretation as the Mechanism of Replication

The study of interpretation is known as *hermeneutics*. A central concept in this tradition is the *hermeneutic circle*: the idea that understanding always moves between parts and wholes. Individuals approach the world with pre‑existing assumptions—what Gadamer calls “prejudices”—and these assumptions shape how new experiences are interpreted. Yet these same assumptions are revised through the act of interpretation itself. Meaning is therefore not static but continually reconstructed.

Contemporary neuroscience offers a complementary perspective. The Thousand Brains Hypothesis proposes that the neocortex does not operate as a single unified model, but as a collection of many parallel cortical columns, each learning its own partial representation of the world. These columns use reference frames to encode relationships between objects, sensations, and concepts, and they integrate their predictions through constant feedback. Ideas, in this framework, are not stored in one location but emerge as distributed patterns across many such columns. This allows us to reinterpret the hermeneutic circle in biological terms: the “prejudices” that shape interpretation correspond to existing distributed models, while new experiences update these models by reorganising the connections between columns.

Ideas replicate not by copying themselves perfectly, but by inducing new configurations in the neural architecture of another mind. Interpretation is therefore the mechanism through which ideas mutate and propagate.

## 1.2 A Simple Model of Intelligence

Intelligence is notoriously difficult to define, but for the purposes of this essay we may describe it as a system that is sensitive to initial conditions and whose emergent behaviour acts to preserve or stabilise certain patterns against disruption. Early replicators in a primordial environment provide a useful analogy. Chemical systems are inherently sensitive to initial conditions: molecules form and decompose continuously, influencing neighbouring molecules in cascading interactions. Within such a system, a replicator is a structure that resists dissolution by producing copies of itself. In this sense, replication is a primitive form of intelligent behaviour—an emergent strategy for maintaining structure in a chaotic environment.

The brain exhibits similar properties. Neural networks are chaotic systems: even simple computational models of neurons can produce complex, unpredictable behaviour when combined. The stable patterns that emerge within this chaos—our assumptions, expectations, and interpretive habits—function as attractors. If these patterns are updated in ways that tend to preserve their overall structure during interpretation, then they behave analogously to intelligent agents. Certain ideas, especially those deeply embedded in a person’s worldview, may therefore act as self‑stabilising informational structures: they resist disruption, shape the interpretation of new information, and replicate by inducing similar structures in other minds.

# Section 2 \- the history of the idea of living ideas

## Introduction

Throughout human history, people have conceptualised ideas, symbols, and deities as if they possessed a kind of life of their own. Long before the language of memetics or cognitive science existed, ancient and esoteric traditions described ideas as agents—forces that could influence behaviour, shape events, and persist across generations. This section examines several of these traditions, not to endorse their metaphysical claims, but to trace how different cultures have understood the agency of ideas. These historical conceptions provide a rich conceptual vocabulary for the memetic structures discussed later in this essay.

## 2.1 Historical background: Rosicrucianism and gnosticism

Early Christianity was not a unified movement but a constellation of competing interpretations. Among these were various Gnostic traditions, which proposed a cosmology involving a transcendent, unknowable source (the Monad), emanations known as Aeons, and a lesser creator figure—the Demiurge—responsible for the material world. While Gnosticism was eventually suppressed by emerging orthodox authorities, its themes resurfaced centuries later in Western esoteric traditions.

Rosicrucianism, which emerged in the early modern period, drew on a mixture of Christian mysticism, Neoplatonism, Hermeticism, and Kabbalah. Although not a direct continuation of ancient Gnosticism, it inherited similar symbolic structures: layered realities, emanations, and the idea that spiritual knowledge (gnosis) transforms the individual. These traditions expanded the notion of a “spiritual plane” and introduced additional conceptual layers such as the “mental plane,” which framed ideas as active forces within a metaphysical hierarchy.

## 2.3 Carl Jung: Archetypes and the Inner Life of Ideas

Carl Jung offers a psychological counterpart to the esoteric and memetic frameworks discussed earlier. In *The Red Book* and his later theoretical writings, Jung explores the idea that the human psyche contains recurring symbolic patterns he called *archetypes*. These archetypes are not inherited images but inherited *forms*—structural tendencies that shape how humans imagine, narrate, and interpret the world. They manifest in myths, dreams, religious symbols, and cultural narratives, giving rise to recurring motifs such as the Hero, the Shadow, the Mother, and the Trickster.

For Jung, these archetypes arise from what he termed the *collective unconscious*: a shared layer of the psyche that transcends individual experience. Although the collective unconscious is not a literal shared mind, it reflects the deep structural similarities in how human brains organise meaning. In this sense, archetypes can be understood as **cognitive attractors**—stable patterns in the architecture of the mind that guide interpretation and behaviour.

This perspective aligns naturally with the memetic framework. If memes are units of cultural transmission, archetypes are the deep templates that shape which memes resonate, replicate, and endure. Mythology, in Jung’s view, is not arbitrary storytelling but the outward expression of these internal patterns. Thus, Jung provides a model in which **living symbols** are not supernatural beings but emergent structures arising from the dynamics of the psyche. They behave like autonomous agents because they influence thought, emotion, and action in ways that feel independent of conscious control.

Jung therefore gives us a way to understand memetic entities—egregores, thoughtforms, gods—not as literal beings but as **expressions of deep psychological structures**. They are the inner life of ideas: the patterns through which the mind organises meaning. 

## 2.4 Hegel: History as the Outer Life of Ideas

Hegel provides a crucial philosophical foundation for understanding ideas as dynamic, evolving structures at the collective level. In the *Phenomenology of Spirit*, he argues that human consciousness develops through a historical process in which ideas, practices, and social forms unfold over time. Hegel’s concept of *Geist*—often translated as “Spirit”—describes this collective dimension of thought. Like Jung’s collective unconscious, Spirit is not a supernatural entity but a structural reality: the totality of a society’s concepts, values, institutions, and symbolic forms.

For Hegel, history is the story of Spirit coming to understand itself. Ideas do not remain static; they evolve through a dialectical process in which contradictions generate new forms of understanding. Each historical epoch embodies a particular configuration of ideas, which eventually encounters its limits and gives rise to a new configuration. In this sense, ideas behave like living structures: they grow, transform, and reorganise themselves in response to internal tensions and external pressures.

A central moment in Hegel’s account is the emergence of self‑consciousness through *recognition*. Individuals become aware of themselves not merely through introspection but through their relations with others. This relational model parallels the way ideas exist not within a single mind but across many minds, sustained through shared interpretation. Just as self‑consciousness arises through mutual recognition, cultural ideas persist through the continual recognition and reinterpretation of a community.

Hegel’s view of history as a self‑developing process anticipates modern theories of cultural evolution. If Spirit is understood as the collective structure of ideas within a society, then historical change can be seen as the transformation of large‑scale memetic complexes. Patterns in history—shifts in religion, morality, political organisation—reflect the internal dynamics of these idea‑structures as they adapt to new circumstances. In this sense, Hegel provides the outer counterpart to Jung’s inner archetypes: ideas form interconnected systems that evolve over time, shaping and being shaped by the societies in which they live.

## 2.5 Language

Why are memetic entities unique to humans? One answer lies in language. The Sapir‑Whorf hypothesis suggests that language influences how individuals categorise and interpret the world. While people can perceive colours regardless of vocabulary, linguistic categories shape how they conceptualise and remember them. A culture without a distinct word for “orange,” for example, may classify it as a shade of red or yellow, altering the conceptual boundaries within which ideas form.

Language, therefore, provides the medium in which interpretation occurs. It structures the memetic landscape, determining which distinctions are salient, which ideas can be expressed, and how they can evolve. In this sense, language is not merely a tool for communication but a substrate for the replication and transformation of ideas.

Just as numbers abstract quantities and geometry abstracts space, we can construct a mathematical framework that abstracts the behaviour of ideas. The goal of this section is not to produce a complete formal system, but to outline the foundations of a model capable of representing memetic structures—thoughtforms, egregores, archetypes—and the interpretive processes through which they evolve and using this model to evaluate the impact of ideas on governing society

## 3.1 Representing Ideas: The Geometry of Memetic Structures

Earlier sections described ideas as distributed neural patterns and memetic entities as clusters of mutually reinforcing memes. To model these structures mathematically, we need a representation that captures:

* **recurrence** (how often a meme appears)  
* **adjacency** (which memes co‑occur)  
* **structure** (how memes relate to one another)  
* **self‑reference** (thoughtforms)  
* **hierarchy** (egregores)

A simple but powerful starting point is to treat a text, concept, or cultural artefact as a **sequence of tokens**, and to analyse the relationships between these tokens.

**Reference Index Representation**

Instead of focusing on the words themselves, we assign each unique token a numerical index based on its first appearance. For example:

“I have an apple. The apple is red.”

becomes:

1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 4, 7, 8

with a dictionary mapping:

{"I":1, "have":2, "an":3, "apple":4, ".":5, "The":6, "is":7, "red":8}

The meaning of the numbers is irrelevant; what matters is the **pattern of recurrence and adjacency**

**Thoughtforms as Cycles**

A *thoughtform* can be defined as a token that participates in a cycle within the sequence graph.  
 In the example above:

* 4 → 5 → 6 → 4 is a cycle  
* therefore “apple” is a thoughtform

Cycles represent self‑reinforcing ideas: concepts that return to themselves through interpretation.

**Egregores as Networks of Thoughtforms**

An *egregore* can be defined as a **connected subgraph** of thoughtforms that co‑occur frequently and form a stable cluster.  
 In other words:

* thoughtform \= loop  
* egregore \= network of loops  
* archetype \= recurrent network across many texts  
* godform \= interface node connecting an egregore to behaviour or ritual

For example the egregore in our sentence 1,2,3,4,5,6,4,7,8 would for now just be a set containing the number 4 which we could call an egregore set

This gives us a geometric interpretation of memetic structures.
